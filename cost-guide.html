Excavation Costs in Lehigh Acres, FL

Most residential excavation jobs run $1,500–$6,300, and digging alone typically costs $2.50–$15 per cubic yard. This guide breaks down every kind of digging job, what moves the price, and how to read a quote — so you know a fair price when you see one.

Home › Cost Guide

First, a note on the numbers: these are typical ranges, not quotes.

Every dig is different. The ranges below come from published national cost guides, applied to residential work in Southwest Florida. Your actual price depends on how deep the dig goes, how much dirt moves, whether the hole hits water, and how far the spoil travels. Use these numbers to compare quotes — not as a quote.

How the per-yard rate works

Excavation volume is measured in cubic yards — a cube of dirt three feet on each side. A 2,000-square-foot footprint dug three feet deep moves roughly 220 cubic yards. At the digging rate alone ($2.50–$15 per cubic yard), that's $550–$3,300 — but that is only the digging. If the dirt leaves the site, hauling adds $8–$25 per cubic yard, which on the same 220 yards is another $1,760–$5,500. That is why two quotes for the same hole can be thousands apart: one includes the haul, the other doesn't. Always ask whether the per-yard figure in a quote covers digging only or digging plus hauling.

Soil type moves the per-yard rate too. Light, sandy soil — which is most of Lehigh Acres — digs at the low end, around $2.50–$6 per cubic yard. Average soil runs $6–$10. Heavy, wet, or rocky ground runs $10–$15, and rock that needs breaking out with a jackhammer or hydraulic hammer can run $50–$200 per cubic yard. Lehigh Acres is mostly sand, so rock is uncommon — but limestone does show up in pockets of Southwest Florida, and a quote should say what happens if the bucket hits it.

What moves the price up (and down)

Depth and volume — the biggest factor

Volume grows fast with depth. Doubling the depth of a dig roughly doubles the material moved — and deep holes also need wider openings, sloped or shored walls, and more time. A shallow footing trench is a small job; a full foundation dig is a large one, even on the same lot. When you describe your project, depth is the single most useful number you can give a contractor.

Groundwater and dewatering

Lehigh Acres' water table sits close to the surface, so deeper digs often hit water. Dewatering — pumping the hole dry while work goes on — means pumps, hoses, discharge, and time. It is routine here, but it is a real cost, and it is the most common reason a local quote comes in above the per-yard rate. A quote that doesn't mention water at all is either assuming a shallow dig or hasn't thought about it.

Hauling distance

Spoil that stays on the lot costs nothing to move. Spoil that leaves costs $8–$25 per cubic yard, and the number climbs with the distance to the disposal site. On a big dig, hauling can cost more than the digging itself. If your lot has room to spread or stockpile spoil, say so — it can save real money.

Fill for low lots

Many Lehigh Acres lots sit low and need imported fill to reach building elevation. Fill runs $6–$17 per cubic yard before spreading, and a pad that needs a foot of fill across a 2,000-square-foot footprint takes roughly 75 cubic yards. Get the fill quantity in writing — it is the most common source of price surprises in site work.

Access and working room

A wide-open lot with a clear entrance is fast work. A tight lot with a narrow access point, fences close to the dig, or overhead lines nearby takes longer and may need a smaller machine. Wet-season ground (May–October) can slow everything down on saturated sandy soil, and rain delays add days to a schedule.

Rock

Rock is uncommon in Lehigh Acres' sandy soil, but limestone pockets exist in Southwest Florida. Rock that needs a hammer to break out runs $50–$200 per cubic yard — an order of magnitude more than digging sand. A good quote will say how rock is handled and priced if it shows up, rather than leaving it as an open-ended extra.

What a quote should cover — and what often isn't in it

Before you compare two quotes, check that each one covers these items:

  • Permitting — Lee County permits, if needed. Sometimes included, sometimes billed separately.
  • Florida 811 locates — the contractor normally arranges these; make sure the job includes them.
  • Dewatering — pumps and time if the dig hits groundwater.
  • Hauling and disposal — spoil hauled away, or reused on site?
  • Fill — how many cubic yards, at what rate, delivered and spread?
  • Backfill and compaction — for trenches and foundation digs, how the ground is put back.
  • Surface restoration — sod, driveway patch, or landscaping after the work.

Two quotes can differ by thousands of dollars and both be fair — if they're quoting different scopes. A lower number only saves money when the scope matches — compare what's inside each quote, not just the total.

How to compare quotes fairly

Get at least two quotes, and make sure each one answers the same questions: how the job is priced, whether hauling and fill are in the price, what happens if the dig hits water or rock, and how long the job takes. A quote that doesn't ask about depth, water, and where the spoil goes is guessing — and guesses get revised upward once the machine is on site.

Ways to keep the cost down

  • Keep spoil on site when the lot and the plan allow it — hauling is often the largest single line on a big dig.
  • Dig in the dry season when schedules allow — wet-season water means more dewatering and more delays.
  • Bundle the work — site excavation, foundation digging, and trenching done in one mobilization share the machine time and the site setup.
  • Know your depth before you ask — a project with plans and a known depth gets a firmer quote than one priced on a guess.
  • Clear the access — a clear path for the machine and the trucks saves time, and time is what hourly billing charges for.
